Surface

The terminal

Emma gives you two different surfaces. One is a full-screen application on the alternate screen, with a sidebar, a retained conversation buffer, a pinned input dock and a status bar. The other is a stream of lines with no escape byte in it. You get the plain stream whenever the full-screen path cannot, and most of the rules in this subsystem sit on the seam between them and on giving your terminal back exactly as it was found.

The shape of it

crates/emma/src/term/ is seventeen files and 16,542 lines, plus the term.rs facade above them and usertools.rs beside them. It is the region the architecture map calls “the largest single gap in this document”, having read “almost nothing” of it. That map also puts the size at “16 files, ~13,000 lines”, which is low on both counts while its own sentence goes on to list seventeen filenames. certified: wc -l crates/emma/src/term/*.rs reports 17 files and 16,542 lines at 62ab9c3.

One shape recurs across it: the modules that decide take no terminal and hold no lock, and the modules that touch a console stay small. Four of the seventeen reach outside the process at all: frame.rs owns the terminal, input.rs reads stdin, statusline.rs starts a task that runs somebody's program, and theme.rs reads a file. The other thirteen are pure over a Buffer, a Vec<Line> or plain data. That's why almost everything here has a test. app.rs gives the reason for keeping the split where it is: “the terminal, the locks and the entry/leave bytes stay in frame.rs, which has already shipped two invisible-to-tests defects and does not need a third file's worth of chances.”

Two facts I want you to carry into the subpages. First, the reversal: for the whole life of this module the rule was Viewport::Inline only, never the alternate screen, and on 2026-08-12 I reversed it, on the ground that the multi-pane layout I specified cannot be drawn inline. Everything about a retained transcript, a sidebar and mouse capture dates from that day, and frame.rs records the outcome without softening it: “That argument was true and it lost.” Second, where I had a choice I took the plain path. I would rather give you a terminal that is correct than one that is pretty and broken, so every gate in the subsystem refuses rather than guesses.

Three constraints without exception

  1. The plain fallback never touches the alternate screen. The enter sequence is one constant written at one place inside Frame::install, and a run with no Frame has no other emitter. See the frame.
  2. Every exit path leaves it: ordinary return, a Drop during unwind, a panic, Ctrl-C, and the one process::exit route. What that cannot cover is named rather than glossed: SIGKILL, a stack overflow that skips the hook, and std::process::abort.
  3. EMMA_UI=inline is the escape hatch, kept for one release as a soak period, tested narrowly enough that an unset variable is the full-screen app.

Four names that overlap

render.rs, view.rs, statusbar.rs and statusline.rs sound interchangeable and are not. render.rs is the vocabulary: an event becomes Lines, with no Rect and no Buffer in sight, and it also owns the inline status row. view.rs is the inline viewport's layout: it takes a rectangle and arranges the box, menu, panel, hint and status row. statusbar.rs is the full-screen successor to that status row and names the inheritance itself: “it inherits that row's one law: everything on it is true.” statusline.rs is somebody else's program, a configured script whose first line replaces whichever built-in row is in play.

The full-screen render path is frame.rs → app.rs → sidebar / chat / statusbar, plus two borrowed methods from view.rs for the dock; View::render itself is not on it. The inline path is frame.rs → View::render. Both consume render.rs.

What the alternate screen cost

The full-screen TUI design prices every cost, including the one line where the flip is a gain: the “a fragment can never be extended” streaming constraint disappears, because an owned buffer can restyle and reflow the entry being streamed on every frame. Three of the costs reach a user.

Five more modules under term/

The table above predates them and does not list them. All five are read for this section and none has a page of its own in this chapter yet, so what follows is one paragraph each rather than the file-by-file treatment the rest of the chapter gets. certified as present: ls crates/emma/src/term/ shows all five, and wc -l gives the line counts quoted below.

crates/emma/src/term/bindings.rs, 1,104 lines, is one table per surface holding the chord, what it does, and proof that something answers it. It exists because a QUICK HELP panel on a branch about to be merged advertised six keys of which four did nothing, and nobody wrote those rows dishonestly: the panel was a hand-written list of (key, description) pairs and the bindings were an exhaustive match in another file. The rule here is that the declaration is the thing that draws. Table::hints is what the sidebar's quick help renders, and it derives the printed label from the Chord rather than taking a second string, so a panel cannot spell a key differently from the key it means. Liveness is a test rather than a type, because Rust cannot ask a match which arms it has: every_drawn_key_is_answered drives each chord through the real decoders and fails on anything that comes back Action::Ignore. The other direction is pinned too. UNDRAWN records keys that work and are not shown, with the reason, and two tests fail when a handled key appears in neither table or when an UNDRAWN row's key stops working. resolve is a second statement of the reader loop's precedence rather than the loop itself: it calls the same four decoders in the same order, so a change to what a key does is picked up and only a change to the order could drift. unverified Nothing here presses a key on a real console, and the module says so. Only a person at a terminal can confirm that the terminal emits the chords these tables name.

crates/emma/src/term/bindings.rs — Table::hints, resolve, UNDRAWN, Chord

crates/emma/src/term/settings.rs, 2,333 lines, draws the settings screen: a title block with the version in the corner, numbered cards in a grid, each card a bordered box of Label … value rows with the value right-aligned. Its own earlier policy is reversed in the file, which is the part worth carrying. It used to render the mock's sample value for a field with no backing state, so Max Context Tokens 8192 was painted identically to a live Model. Every row now ends in one of three visually distinct states: live and editable, live and read-only with a notice naming where it is changed, or absent, drawn dim and reading n/a or not set rather than a plausible placeholder. The dim-against-accent split carries that, because colour is the only channel a Label … value row has left for a reader who presses nothing. Interaction is two pure halves: handle_key mutates focus and returns a SettingsAction for anything needing disk or network, and the mouse path records every control's rect from the same arithmetic that painted it, then dispatches a click through the same key path, so the two cannot disagree about what a control does. unverified Which rows are live today is a claim about the file's own list and was not checked against a running binary. Opening the screen and pressing each row would settle it.

crates/emma/src/term/settings.rs — Value, handle_key, render_hits, hit

crates/emma/src/term/inspect.rs, 1,669 lines, and crates/emma/src/term/rungraph.rs, 2,867 lines, are two harness views drawn from the owner's mocks. Inspect is a head block, a seven-cell header strip, and cards for step timeline, run metadata, tool calls, artifacts and event log. Rungraph is a box-drawn execution DAG on the left, a selected-node inspector on the right, a five-panel strip beneath, and a command bar. Both share one divergence from their mocks, and it is the data: the harness they describe does not exist yet, so both render from a view type. A populated view reproduces the mock and the live view starts with no run. inspect.rs with run: None draws the head and one dim line and withholds the input bar, because every cell on the page describes one run and empty chrome would claim a run exists. rungraph.rs keeps the chrome, says there is no run graph in the canvas, and shows real zeros and dashes where a value would be a claim; its legend still renders in full because it explains vocabulary rather than claiming state. Rungraph owns its own layout: layered top-down with a node's layer one past its deepest parent, uniform boxes, siblings centred per layer, three gap rows between layers for the dashed edges, and no force direction. Both are pure rendering. Keys and mounting belong to the shell. unverified Neither page has been seen against a real harness, because there is no real harness. What would settle the populated face is a run that produces the records these views read.

crates/emma/src/term/inspect.rs — InspectView, EMPTY_PAGE; crates/emma/src/term/rungraph.rs — GraphView, GraphView::scroll

crates/emma/src/term/welcome.rs is 211 lines and is the one module here with nothing aspirational in it. Emma had no first run at all: a new user saw a warning, two paths and an empty box, with nothing naming the tools that had just been loaded on their behalf. Everything on the block is read from the run that is starting, the harness that loaded, the tools that survived selection, the commands that exist in this project, because a welcome listing capabilities the run does not have teaches first that Emma's account of itself cannot be trusted. It is shown once, and which of the two detection reasons was used is printed on the block, since a returning user's first question is why they are seeing it. certified: Welcome has a reason field and Skin::welcome renders it.

What a cell buffer cannot settle

Whether any of this looks right on your console is the one thing no test in this tree can answer. A fully-tested scrollback defect has shipped from this directory twice; both times a green suite was the evidence, and both times it was wrong the same way, because a TestBackend agrees with whoever wrote the fixture. app.rs puts it as frame.rs having “already shipped two invisible-to-tests defects.” Every page in this chapter closes with what it could not settle, and the largest items are collected on what is not verified.

The emma library suite is 477 tests and 3 ignored, green at 62ab9c3. The three ignored ones were run for the user-tools page and are the only certification against a real machine anywhere in this subsystem. Nothing in this chapter rests on the green suite alone.
